The puzzle behind Knighted!

What is the Knight's Tour?

One chess knight. Every square of the board. No square twice. A puzzle people have been solving for more than a thousand years.

The knight's tour is one of the oldest puzzles in recreational mathematics. It needs nothing but a chessboard and a single knight, yet it has fascinated poets, chess masters, mathematicians and computer scientists for more than a thousand years. Here is how it works, where it came from, and how to solve one yourself.

How a chess knight moves

A knight moves in an L-shape: two squares in one direction, then one square to the side. It is the only chess piece that jumps, so nothing in between can block it. Every jump also changes the colour of the square it stands on, from light to dark or dark to light.

That difference is the key to the whole puzzle. A square in the middle can be reached from eight others, a square on the edge from three or four, and a corner from just two.

The challenge: every square, exactly once

A knight's tour is a route that lands the knight on every square of the board exactly once. Start anywhere, keep jumping, and never return to a square you have already visited. On a standard 8×8 board, that is 63 jumps without a single mistake.

The first twenty moves feel easy. Then the board fills up, and the knight starts running out of places to go. Corners are the trap: if you use up both of a corner's two neighbours without visiting the corner, it is lost for good, and you may not find out until thirty moves later.

There is no shortage of answers. Mathematicians have counted more than 13 trillion different closed tours on a standard board alone, and far more open ones. The difficulty is that there are vastly more ways to go wrong.

Open vs closed tours

If the knight's last square is one knight's move from where it started, so it could jump straight back and go round again, the tour is called closed. Otherwise it is open. Here are one of each on a small 6×6 board, with the first and last squares ringed in gold:

A closed tour is a loop, so you can start it from any of its 64 squares. Closed tours are harder to find, and some boards have none at all. A 5×5 board has open tours but no closed one, and no board with an odd number of squares can be closed: the knight changes colour on every jump, so a loop needs as many light squares as dark ones.

History

  • Around 840. Tours appear in early Arabic writing on shatranj, the ancestor of chess. One is credited to al-Adli ar-Rumi, a shatranj master who wrote one of the game's earliest books, and his is a closed tour. It survives in a later manuscript.
  • Around 900, India. The Sanskrit poet Rudrata, in his book on poetics Kavyalankara, wrote verses whose syllables can be read by following a knight's path over half a chessboard. It is one of the earliest known knight's tours.
  • Before Euler, India. The scholar Nilakantha recorded tours, including symmetrical ones, at least sixty years before Euler took up the problem.
  • 1759. The mathematician Leonhard Euler wrote Solution of a curious question which does not seem to be subject to any analysis for the Berlin Academy, printed in 1766. It was one of the first mathematical studies of the puzzle, showing ways to build new tours from old ones and to complete partial tours.
  • Late 1700s. The Turk, a famous chess-playing "machine" that was secretly worked by a hidden player, performed a closed knight's tour for its audiences.
  • 1823. H. C. von Warnsdorff published a simple rule for finding a tour by hand, still the best-known trick for the puzzle (see below).
  • 1990s. Allen Schwenk worked out exactly which rectangular boards have a closed tour (1991), and computers finally counted every closed tour on the standard board (Löbbing and Wegener, 1996, corrected by Brendan McKay in 1997).
  • Today. The knight's tour is a classic example in computer science courses and puzzle books, and a favourite test for search algorithms.

Mathematical connections

Draw a dot for each of the 64 squares, and a line between any two squares a knight's move apart. Mathematicians call this the knight's graph. On an 8×8 board it has 64 dots and 168 lines, too many to draw clearly, so here is the graph of a 5×5 board.

A knight's tour is then a Hamiltonian path on that graph: a route along the lines that visits every dot exactly once. A closed tour is a Hamiltonian cycle, a path whose two ends are joined. Finding Hamiltonian paths in a general network is one of the famously hard problems of computer science, with no fast method known.

The knight's graph is a happy exception. It has so much regular structure that tours can be built very quickly, in time that grows only in step with the number of squares, by splitting a big board into small pieces, touring each piece and stitching the pieces together (Conrad and colleagues, 1994; Parberry, 1997).

Solving strategies

Warnsdorff's rule

Always jump to the square that has the fewest onward moves left.

The idea is to visit the hard-to-reach squares first, while you still can, and leave the well-connected middle for later. Warnsdorff's rule is a heuristic: a rule of thumb that usually works, not a guaranteed solution.

  1. List the moves. Three moves in, the knight is on c1. It can jump to a2, d3 or e2.
  2. Count onward moves. For each one, count the unvisited squares the knight could jump to from there: a2 has 2, d3 has 7 and e2 has 5.
  3. Pick the fewest. Jump to a2. With only 2 ways out, it is the square most at risk of being cut off, so visit it now.
  4. Repeat. Do the same from each new square until the board is full.

Why it can still fail: when two squares tie, the rule does not say which to choose, and a poor choice can run into a dead end, so mathematicians have proposed several ways to break ties. Even so, on a standard board it finds a complete tour remarkably often. Every tour on this page was found with its help.

Backtracking

Try a move. If it leads to a dead end, step back and try another. A computer can do this systematically, but a full board has far too many dead ends to try them all one by one, so backtracking works best combined with a good rule like Warnsdorff's to choose which move to try first.

Divide and conquer, and neural networks

For very large boards, the fastest methods split the board into smaller rectangles, tour each one and join them up. Researchers have even trained neural networks to find tours (Takefuji and Lee, 1992).

Tips for solving by hand

  • Work the edges first. Start on an edge or in a corner, and sweep around the rim before heading for the middle.
  • Never strand a corner. When a corner has only one way in left, go there soon, or it will have to be your very last square.
  • Count the ways in. A square with just one unvisited neighbour can only be your final square, and you can only have one of those.
  • Save the middle for last. Central squares are the easiest to get in and out of, so they are the best squares to finish on.
  • Step back further. When you hit a dead end, the mistake is usually several moves back. Undo a few moves, not just one.

Interesting facts

  • 26,534,728,821,064 closed tours on a standard board, or 13,267,364,410,532 if a tour and the same tour run backwards count as one.
  • 19,591,828,170,979,904 tours in all, open and closed, counting each direction separately.
  • 5×5 is the smallest square board with a tour. The 2×2, 3×3 and 4×4 boards have none, and every rectangle at least 5 squares on each side has at least one (Conrad and colleagues, 1994).
  • Closed tours are picky. A rectangle has one unless both its sides are odd, its shorter side is 1, 2 or 4 squares, or it is 3 squares wide and 4, 6 or 8 long (Schwenk, 1991).
  • No magic tour. A computer search in 2003 showed that no 8×8 tour numbers the board so that every row, column and diagonal adds up to the same total. Tours where just the rows and columns match do exist, and Euler drew one.
  • Beyond the chessboard. Mathematicians study tours on boards of every size and shape, even on a torus, a board whose edges wrap around to meet each other.

Frequently asked questions

Can a knight visit all 64 squares of a standard chessboard exactly once?

Yes. A route that does it is called a knight’s tour, and a standard 8×8 board has trillions of them. Because closed tours exist, a tour can even start on any square you like. More

How many possible Knight’s Tours exist on a standard 8×8 chessboard?

It depends on how you count. There are 26,534,728,821,064 closed tours if each direction counts separately, or 13,267,364,410,532 if a tour and its reverse count as one. Add the open tours and there are 19,591,828,170,979,904 in all, counting each direction. In these figures, a tour that is just another one turned or mirrored still counts as a different tour. More

What is the difference between an open and a closed Knight’s Tour?

In a closed tour the last square is one knight’s move from the first, so the knight could jump straight back and go round again. In an open tour it cannot. Closed tours are harder to find, and some boards have none at all. More

What is Warnsdorff’s Rule, and how does it help solve a Knight’s Tour?

Published by H. C. von Warnsdorff in 1823, it says: always jump to the square with the fewest onward moves. That visits the hard-to-reach squares before they can be cut off. It is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee, but on a standard board it finds a complete tour remarkably often. More

Is the Knight’s Tour difficult to solve?

By hand, it is harder than it looks. The first moves are easy, then corners and edges get cut off, often without you noticing until many moves later. Warnsdorff’s rule and a little practice make it far more manageable, and a computer can build a tour almost instantly. More
